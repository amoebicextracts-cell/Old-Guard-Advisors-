Why Cannabis Facility Launches Slip Six Months

Old Guard Cannabis Advisors
← Back to all posts

Ask an operator six months into construction when they'll be harvesting, and you'll get a confident date. Ask again three months later and the date has moved — not because the build slowed down, but because the build was never the actual critical path. It just looked like it, because it's the part everyone can see.

The delays that actually push a launch back live somewhere less visible: the SOP library nobody started writing until the walls went up, the licensing submission that assumed a faster agency turnaround than the agency has ever delivered, the trained staff nobody hired until there was a room to put them in.

The build finishes. The facility doesn't.

A finished room is not an operational facility. Between "the contractor hands you the keys" and "you're legally allowed to put a plant in a pot," there's a stack of work that has nothing to do with drywall: standard operating procedures written and approved, a quality system stood up, staff hired and trained on procedures that didn't exist a month earlier, and — in most markets — a licensing agency that has to inspect and sign off before anything moves.

Most construction schedules are built by people who are extremely good at building rooms and have never had to wait on a state inspector's calendar. That's not a criticism of the contractor. It's a sequencing gap, and it's predictable enough that it shouldn't still be surprising anyone.

Where the real time goes

The tasks that actually determine your launch date rarely show up on the construction Gantt chart at all:

  • SOP development starts on day one of construction, not week one of operations. Writing a real SOP library — not a template pack — takes months. Start it the day the lease is signed, not the day the paint dries.
  • Licensing lead times are the agency's, not yours. Submit for every required inspection and sign-off as early as the process allows, and build your timeline around their calendar, not your hopeful one.
  • Staff need to be trained before the first batch, not during it. Hiring three weeks before harvest means your team is learning the SOPs and running production simultaneously — that's how deviations happen in week one.
  • Equipment lead times get discovered too late. Long-lead items — climate systems, specialized extraction or processing equipment — need to be ordered against the construction schedule, not after it, or they become the new bottleneck.
  • Utility commissioning has its own sign-off sequence. HVAC and electrical systems often need their own inspection and balancing before anything else can be signed off behind them — confirm that sequence early, not when you're already behind.
The construction schedule tells you when the building will be done. It has never once told you when the facility will be licensed to operate.

Protecting the date you actually announced

The operators who hit their launch date aren't the ones with the fastest contractors. They're the ones who ran two timelines in parallel from day one — construction on one track, licensing/SOPs/staffing on the other — and treated the slower of the two as the real completion date, not the faster one. If your public launch date is driven by the construction schedule alone, it's already wrong; it just hasn't caught up to you yet.

That's not pessimism. It's just which track has actually been the long pole, in every launch I've been part of.
